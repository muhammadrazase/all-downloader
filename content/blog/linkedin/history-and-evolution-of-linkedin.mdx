## From a living room startup to a professional standard

The history of LinkedIn begins in a living room in late 2002, where Reid Hoffman and a small founding team sketched out a network built on trust and professional identity rather than casual friendship. The site launched publicly in May 2003 with a simple promise: your reputation and your connections should follow you, not sit locked inside a single employer's building.

Growth was slow at first. In its opening weeks LinkedIn added only a few thousand members, and many observers doubted that professionals would spend time on a social site. What kept it alive was a genuinely useful idea. Your network had value, and making it visible and searchable turned quiet relationships into real opportunities.

## The early years: identity and the resume

Through the mid-2000s, LinkedIn defined itself as the place where your career lived online. The profile became a public, structured resume. Recommendations added social proof, and the "People You May Know" feature turned dormant contacts into active connections.

Two moves cemented this era. The introduction of a jobs marketplace gave recruiters a reason to pay, and the launch of paid subscriptions created a business model that did not depend entirely on advertising. By the end of the decade LinkedIn was profitable, went public in 2011, and had become the default answer to a simple question: where do serious professionals keep their profile?

## The content pivot: from directory to publisher

The next chapter changed what LinkedIn actually was. A static directory of profiles is useful once in a while, but a place people visit daily needs a reason to return. That reason became content.

LinkedIn opened its publishing platform to all members in the mid-2010s, letting anyone write long-form articles. The feed grew more central, influencers emerged, and professionals began sharing ideas rather than only updating job titles. Microsoft acquired the company in 2016 for around 26 billion dollars, signaling that a professional graph plus a content engine was worth far more than a job board alone.

## The mobile and feed era

As smartphones took over, LinkedIn rebuilt around a mobile feed. Reactions expanded beyond a single "like," native document posts let people share slide decks, and the algorithm began rewarding conversation and dwell time. The platform learned a lesson its consumer rivals already knew. People come back for a stream of relevant content, and they stay for the discussions underneath it.

This period also blurred the line between personal and professional. Members started sharing career setbacks, lessons, and behind-the-scenes stories. The tone loosened, and posts that once would have felt too personal for a work network became some of the most engaged content on the platform.

## The shift to video

By the early 2020s, video was reshaping every major platform, and LinkedIn followed. Native video uploads, live broadcasts, and eventually a dedicated short-form video experience moved motion to the center of the feed. Professionals discovered that a ninety-second clip explaining an idea often outperformed a wall of text.

This is the shift that defines LinkedIn in 2026. Video is no longer an experiment tucked into the corner of the feed. It is a primary format that recruiters, founders, and subject-matter experts use to build authority. ## Why the evolution matters today

Understanding this arc helps you use the platform well. LinkedIn rewards whatever format keeps professionals engaged, and today that format is increasingly video. Creators who adapt early tend to capture outsized reach before the feed saturates, exactly as early article writers did a decade ago.

The platform's core value has stayed remarkably consistent across twenty-three years. Your professional identity and your network are assets, and LinkedIn keeps finding new ways to make them productive. The formats change from resumes to articles to video, but the underlying bet on professional trust has never wavered.

## Frequently asked questions

### When was LinkedIn founded and by whom?

LinkedIn was founded by Reid Hoffman and a founding team including Allen Blue, Konstantin Guericke, Eric Ly, and Jean-Luc Vaillant. The site launched publicly in May 2003, growing slowly at first before becoming the standard professional network.

### Why did LinkedIn move toward video?

Video keeps professionals engaged longer and communicates ideas faster than text. As short-form video reshaped every major platform, LinkedIn added native uploads, live streaming, and a dedicated video feed to keep members returning daily and to give experts a richer way to build authority.

### Who owns LinkedIn now?

Microsoft acquired LinkedIn in 2016 for roughly 26 billion dollars. The platform continues to operate under its own brand while benefiting from Microsoft's scale, and the acquisition reflected the growing value of combining a professional network with a content engine.
