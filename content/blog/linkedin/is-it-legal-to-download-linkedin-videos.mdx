## The honest short answer

People ask whether it is legal to download LinkedIn videos far more than they ask how, and the honest answer is: it depends on what you do with the file. Saving a clip for your own private reference sits in very different territory from reuploading someone else's video as your own. Intent and use decide almost everything.

This guide walks through the practical distinctions so you can act with confidence. It is general information written by a strategist, not legal advice, and if you have a specific commercial situation you should consult a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction.

## Two rulebooks apply at once

Downloading involves two separate sets of rules, and both matter.

The first is **copyright law**. The person who created and uploaded a video generally owns the copyright to it. That ownership exists whether or not the video carries a visible notice, and it governs whether you may copy, share, or reuse the content.

The second is **LinkedIn's Terms of Service**. This is a contract you agreed to when you joined. It sets rules for how you may interact with content on the platform, independent of copyright. You can technically comply with copyright while still breaching the terms, so both need attention.

## When downloading is generally reasonable

Several common uses fall into low-risk territory because they do not redistribute or misrepresent anyone's work.

- **Personal offline viewing.** Saving a talk to watch on a flight, for your own eyes only.
- **Private study.** Analyzing a well-made clip to learn pacing, structure, or delivery.
- **Your own content.** Downloading videos you created and posted yourself, which is unambiguously fine.
- **Archiving with permission.** Keeping a copy of a colleague's video when they have said yes.

## When downloading crosses a line

Risk rises sharply the moment you make someone else's video public or profit from it without permission.

Reuploading a creator's clip to your own feed, stripping their name or watermark, editing it to change its meaning, or using it in paid advertising are all clear problem cases. These can infringe copyright, breach the platform terms, and damage your professional reputation all at once. In a network built entirely on credibility, being caught passing off another person's work is uniquely costly.

Scraping video at scale with automated tools is a separate category of risk. Bulk collection typically violates platform terms outright and can trigger legal exposure well beyond ordinary copyright questions, so it sits far outside safe personal use.

## The permission-and-credit rule

If you remember one principle, make it this: for anything public, get permission and give credit. A short message asking a creator whether you may reshare their clip costs you nothing and protects you completely. Most professionals are glad to say yes when asked and credited.

When you do share with permission, name the original creator clearly and, where possible, link back to their post. This keeps you on the right side of both rulebooks and, just as importantly, builds goodwill in a community that runs on reciprocity. The reputational upside of being generous with credit is real.

## Practical safe-use checklist

Before you save or share a LinkedIn video, run through a few quick questions. Is this for private use or public? Did I create it, or do I have the creator's permission? Am I keeping their name and context intact? Am I avoiding automated bulk collection?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it illegal to download a LinkedIn video for personal use?

Saving a video purely for private offline viewing or personal study is generally low risk, since you are not redistributing it or misrepresenting authorship. Problems arise when you republish, edit the meaning, or commercialize someone else's work without permission. When in doubt, keep it private or ask first.

### Can I repost someone's LinkedIn video if I credit them?

Crediting helps but is not automatic permission. Copyright still belongs to the creator, so the safest path is to ask directly before resharing, then credit clearly and link back. Most professionals agree readily when approached politely, and that quick message removes the risk entirely.

### Does downloading violate LinkedIn's Terms of Service?

It can, depending on method and use. Automated or bulk scraping typically breaches the terms, while a one-off personal save sits in grayer territory. Because the terms operate separately from copyright, review LinkedIn's current policies and avoid any automated large-scale collection.
