## Why subtitles matter more than ever

Learning how to get subtitles from a video used to mean transcribing by hand or paying for a captioning service. Neither is necessary anymore. Modern AI speech recognition can listen to almost any clip and produce accurate, time-stamped captions in a couple of minutes, for free, without you typing a word.

Subtitles are no longer a nice-to-have. They make videos accessible to viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, they let people watch with the sound off in public, and they help non-native speakers follow along at their own pace. For creators and students, captions are also the fastest route to a usable text version of any video, which opens up repurposing, searching and studying that raw footage never allows.

## SRT, VTT and plain text: what the formats mean

When you get subtitles from a video, you will usually see a choice of output. The differences are simple once you know what each one is for.

- **SRT (SubRip):** The most widely supported subtitle format. It is a plain text file that pairs each line of speech with a start and end timestamp. Almost every video player, editor and platform accepts SRT, so it is the safe default when you want captions to appear on screen.
- **VTT (WebVTT):** A close cousin of SRT built for the web. It supports the same timing plus a little extra styling, and it is what browsers and HTML5 players expect. Choose VTT when your captions are headed for a website or web app.
- **Plain text:** No timestamps at all, just the words. This is what you want when you plan to read, quote, summarize or feed the content into another tool rather than display it on the video.

A useful rule of thumb: pick SRT for video editing and uploads, VTT for the web, and plain text when you only care about the words themselves.

## Editing an SRT file cleanly

AI transcription is strong, but no tool is flawless. Background noise, heavy accents, crosstalk and unusual proper nouns can all cause the occasional slip, so a quick pass pays off.

An SRT file is just plain text, which means you can open it in any basic text editor. Each caption block has a number, a timestamp line and the spoken words. When you fix a typo or a misheard name, change only the words and leave the numbers and timestamps untouched, since those control when the caption appears. Save the file with its original `.srt` extension and it is ready to upload or import.

If you plan to reuse the words rather than display them, exporting to plain text first saves effort. You get a clean transcript with no timestamps to strip out, which is ideal when you want to download a video transcript for notes, quotes or a summary.
